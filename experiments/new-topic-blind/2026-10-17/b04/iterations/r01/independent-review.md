## Theory and proof

The sign and direction of the new ironing argument are correct. With lower quantile (x=F_g(v)), DSIC makes (q_i(x)) nondecreasing. Writing (D_g=H_g-G_g\ge0), the proof obtains (\int\widetilde s_gq_i-\int s_gq_i=\int D_g\,dq_i\ge0) (Appendix A, equation labeled `ironinginequality`). The endpoint terms vanish because the greatest convex minorant contacts (H_g) at zero and one. Its affine pieces on ({D_g>0}) make the constructed agent's quality constant on every strict ironing interval. The fixed, report-independent tie rule is essential to that equality. Sorting the ironed scores then maximizes the pointwise upper bound for unequal qualities. This establishes optimality over randomized competitors as well as deterministic ones, provided the conditioning step uses the asserted group law.

**Conditional distribution needs a sharper assumption (Section 2; Appendix A, virtual-welfare and ironing proofs).** The manuscript says that type pairs are independent *conditional on the vector of group labels*, but defines (f_g) and (\mu_g) conditional only on agent (i)'s own label. Conditional independence alone does not imply that the law of ((v_i,w_i)) is unchanged when the designer observes *other* agents' labels. The proofs explicitly rely on that stronger statement when fixing opponents' reports. For example, let agent 1 always have label A, and let agent 2's observable label be B or C with equal probability. Give both independent uniform values, set (w_2=1), and set (w_1=0) under B and (w_1=4) under C. Type pairs are independent conditional on the full label vector, but (\mu_A=2) conditional only on A. With (\lambda=0) and one slot, the stated group-mean rule assigns it to agent 1 in both label states; conditional on B, giving it to agent 2 for free is strictly better. State that the full conditional joint law factors as (\prod_i P_{g_i}(dv_i,dw_i)), or let the scores depend on the entire observed label vector. If labels are treated as a fixed vector throughout, say so explicitly and define the conditional means accordingly.

**Endpoint reports need a convention (Section 6, definition of (\widetilde S_g); Proposition 4).** The rule defines the ironed score as a right derivative at (F_g(v)), yet a right derivative is undefined at the admissible upper report (v=\bar v_g). It can also diverge at a boundary under the stated finite-mean assumptions. For instance, with (f(v)=2(1-v)), (\lambda=0), and deterministic (w=(1-v)^{-3/2}) for (v<1), mean weight is finite and the regular raw score is (S(v)=2/\sqrt{1-v}\to+\infty). Thus the issue is not merely a choice among finite derivative values. Define endpoint scores by one-sided extended-real limits and specify their fixed-priority ranking, or directly extend the allocation monotonically at endpoint reports. These reports have zero prior mass but remain in the DSIC domain. The convex-envelope lemma should also briefly justify continuity at the endpoints of this particular minorant before invoking absolute continuity; the claim is true here, but does not follow from finite convexity alone.

The revision fixes my previous wording concern. Section 3 now distinguishes weakly supported truthful weight talk from outcome equivalence almost everywhere, and the conclusion no longer claims literal impossibility of a truthful utterance. The virtual-surplus identity, regular-case ranking, subsidy calculation, and examples remain persuasive. In particular, the fully decreasing result correctly identifies a report-independent, zero-charge allocation by (M_i=\mathbb E[w_iv_i\mid g_i]), subject to the conditional-law clarification above.

## Contribution and recommendation

Proposition 4 gives the manuscript a complete allocation characterization within its chosen model, which is a substantial improvement over the original regular-only result. Still, the proof follows the familiar envelope, convex-minorant ironing, and assignment-by-swap sequence. The manuscript cites only Myerson (1981) and Saez and Stantcheva (2016), and expressly declines to establish historical priority or novelty (Introduction and Discussion). From those two references alone I cannot say the result is known, but I also cannot judge what distinct advance it makes over the existing mechanism-design literature. The remaining application is deliberately narrow: independent one-dimensional values, a known joint prior and welfare criterion, rival slots, and no cap on charges. The paper offers no identification or institutional application that would make the result consequential beyond that class.

I therefore retain a negative recommendation for *JPE*. I would welcome the endpoint and conditional-law repairs in any version, but they are not the main obstacle. Publication at this journal would require a convincing literature position and a substantive new economic insight or extension beyond the standard ironing framework. This recommendation does not rest on a demonstrated failure of the central Stieltjes ironing argument.
